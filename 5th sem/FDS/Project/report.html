1. Introduction

1.1 Project title and overview

Late Delivery Risk Prediction in E-Commerce Logistics. The project predicts, at the moment an order is accepted, whether that order will miss its promised delivery date. It uses the E-Commerce Delivery & Shipping Data 2026 dataset (50,000 orders, 30 columns) and is delivered as a single Jupyter notebook (CT24084.ipynb) plus the raw and cleaned CSV files.

1.2 Problem statement

Every online order carries a promise — a number of days within which it should arrive. When the actual delivery time exceeds that promise, the customer is disappointed, support costs rise and the return rate increases. A fulfilment team cannot inspect 50,000 orders by hand, but it can act on a ranked risk list: upgrade the service level, switch carrier, or notify the customer before the promise is broken.

The question this project answers is therefore: given only information that exists before the parcel leaves the warehouse, can we predict whether an order will be delivered late?

1.3 Objectives and expected outcome

  1. Understand the dataset: its features, its target, and the relationships between them.
  2. Diagnose and repair data-quality problems, and document every decision.
  3. Build a leak-free preprocessing pipeline that is fitted on training data only.
  4. Train and compare several classification models using cross-validation.
  5. Tune the most promising model and evaluate it once on untouched test data.

Expected outcome: a model that separates late from on-time orders clearly better than the majority-class baseline, with a reported ROC-AUC around 0.90, and a documented explanation of which features drive the prediction.

1.4 Why machine learning is suitable

The outcome is not the product of one variable. Weather, order priority, distance, the promised service level and warehouse processing time all contribute, and — as Section 3 shows — they interact: a storm is far worse on a 6,000 km route than on a 500 km one. A hand-written rule would need a branch for every combination of these conditions and would still miss combinations unseen in the historical data.

There are 40,000 training orders, enough to estimate that structure, and the target is binary, so this is a standard supervised classification problem with a clear, measurable answer. Finally, the output is a probability per order, which is directly actionable: the team can rank orders and act on the top decile rather than on a single yes/no cut.

Binary classification. late_delivery is categorical, so regression metrics (MAE, MSE, RMSE, R²) do not apply; accuracy, precision, recall, F1, ROC-AUC and the confusion matrix are used instead. The notebook states this explicitly rather than silently omitting the regression metrics.

3.4 Key findings from exploration

  1. Weather and priority dominate; distance is the backbone numeric feature.
  2. The two strongest effects compound. Storm at 5,000+ km is 100% late (493 of 493); Clear below 1,000 km is 16.7%. A main-effects-only model would average over exactly the cells that matter most.
  3. Four categorical columns are statistically null: product_category (p = 0.970), customer_segment (p = 0.632), package_size (p = 0.341) and payment_method (p = 0.216).
  4. There is no seasonality in the target: the monthly late rate varies only between 55.5% and 57.7%, and the weather mix does not follow the calendar either — about 10% of July orders are snow-affected. Month therefore adds nothing to the model.
  5. The target is perfectly reconstructible from post-delivery columns: late_delivery equals actual_delivery_days > promised_delivery_days on 50,000 of 50,000 rows.

4.2 Cleaning decisions and their reasoning

The cleaned file is 50,000 × 19 with zero missing values. Three decisions deserve explanation:

  • Outliers were not removed. The standard 1.5 × IQR rule would delete 11.85% of rows carrying more than half of all revenue, while their late rate is identical to the rest. That is the textbook case where mechanical outlier removal destroys signal. Heavy tails are handled by scaling and by tree models instead.
  • The $500 cost ceiling was flagged, not erased. Re-estimating censored prices would invent data; deleting them would remove the most informative long-haul orders. A binary flag lets the model treat capped shipments separately.
  • Future-dated and unfinished-status rows were kept. Dropping them would delete 14.2% and 11.6% of the data respectively to fix columns that either do not enter the model (status) or are not raw features (date). Both choices are documented in the notebook.

4.3 Handling categorical and numerical features

Numerical features are median-imputed and standardised. The median is used because the numeric features are right-skewed (Section 3.1), and the median is unaffected by the extreme tail that the mean is dragged towards — for order value the mean is $177.92 against a median of $76.84. Standardisation (z-scores) is required by logistic regression and harmless for trees; min–max scaling was rejected because the maximum order value is 2.43× its 99th percentile, which would push 76.7% of orders into the bottom 5% of the transformed range.

Categorical features are most-frequent-imputed and one-hot encoded. All of them are nominal — a label encoding would invent a ranking such as “Snow > Rain”. handle_unknown="ignore" guarantees that a category unseen in training produces a zero row instead of an error at prediction time.

Both transforms live inside a scikit-learn ColumnTransformer inside a Pipeline, so they are refitted from scratch on every cross-validation fold and on the final fit. No statistic from the validation or test rows is ever used.

4.4 Feature engineering and selection

Three row-wise features were added, each tied to a finding from Section 3:

Feature Definition Rationale
weather_severity Clear 0 → Snow 5 Turns six unordered levels into the observed risk gradient
priority_rank Urgent 0 → Low 3 Same, for the priority queue
distance_per_promised_day km ÷ max(promise, 1) How much ground each promised day must cover

order_date was dropped rather than decomposed into month and weekday, because the exploration showed no seasonal pattern to capture.

Selection uses SelectKBest with mutual information, fitted on training data only, keeping 15 encoded columns. Mutual information is preferred to a correlation filter because it captures non-linear dependence — the multiplicative weather × distance relationship is exactly the case where Pearson correlation understates a feature’s value.

4.5 Important preprocessing decisions, summarised

Decision Why
Split before any transformation Otherwise the scaler, imputer and selector see test rows
Imputation inside the pipeline Guarantees train-only statistics in every fold
Median rather than mean imputation Skewed distributions; the median ignores the extreme tail
One-hot rather than ordinal encoding Nominal categories without inherent order
StandardScaler rather than MinMaxScaler Extreme order values would compress the useful range
Mutual-information selection, k = 15 Removes redundant dummies while keeping the strong non-linear signals
No resampling 56.3/43.7 is a mild imbalance (1.3:1); class weighting was tested and did not improve ranking

5. Machine Learning Methodology

5.1 Train–test split

Item Value
Method train_test_split
Test size 20% (10,000 orders)
Stratification stratify=y — both halves keep the 56.3% late rate
Random seed random_state=42
Training rows 40,000 (late rate 56.33%)
Test rows 10,000 (late rate 56.32%)

The split happens before imputation, scaling, encoding or selection. This is the single most important line of defence against leakage: if a scaler is fitted on all 50,000 rows, the training set has already seen the test set’s means and variances.

A stratified random split is appropriate because this is not a time series. Orders are independent events, and the late rate is flat across all twelve months, so a temporal split would not create a meaningfully different test set. The test set is used exactly once, at the end.

5.2 Algorithms

Model How it works Key settings
Logistic regression Computes a weighted sum of the inputs and maps it to a probability with the logistic function; the decision boundary is linear in the input features. Fitted by maximising the likelihood. max_iter=1000
Decision tree Recursively splits the training data at the threshold that most reduces impurity (Gini), producing a set of if/else rules. max_depth=8, min_samples_leaf=20
Random forest Fits many trees on bootstrap samples of the rows, each considering a random subset of features at every split, then averages their predicted probabilities. 200 trees, min_samples_leaf=5
Gradient boosting Fits trees sequentially, each trained on the residual error of the current ensemble, with a learning rate controlling each tree’s contribution. 200 iterations, learning_rate=0.1

All four are wrapped in the identical preprocessing pipeline, so the comparison isolates the model and nothing else.

5.3 Cross-validation

Five-fold stratified cross-validation on the 40,000 training rows only (StratifiedKFold(n_splits=5, shuffle=True, random_state=42)). Stratification preserves the class ratio in every fold; shuffling prevents the folds from following the original row order. Every metric in the comparison table is the mean of five fold scores, and the fold-to-fold standard deviation is reported so that differences can be judged against noise.

5.4 Hyperparameter tuning

GridSearchCV on the gradient-boosting pipeline, scoring ROC-AUC, using the same five folds:

Parameter Values searched What it controls
learning_rate 0.05, 0.10 How much each new tree contributes
max_leaf_nodes 15, 31 How complex each tree may be

Four configurations × five folds = 20 pipeline fits, each refitting imputation, encoding and feature selection from scratch. The search never sees the test data.

5.5 How leakage is prevented, step by step

Stage Data it may use
Imputation, scaling, encoding, selection fitted inside the pipeline → training rows of the current fold only
Cross-validation training rows only
Grid search training rows only, through the same folds
Model selection CV score on training rows only
Test evaluation once, after every decision is fixed

Because the selector is a pipeline step, a fold’s validation rows cannot influence which features are kept. This closes the most common leak in feature-selection workflows.

6. Model Evaluation and Comparison

6.1 Metrics and why they are appropriate

  • Accuracy — share of correct predictions. Meaningful here because the classes are close to balanced, but insufficient on its own: a model can be accurate while missing most late orders.
  • Precision — of the orders flagged late, how many really are. High precision means few false alarms (unnecessary upgrades).
  • Recall — of the orders that are actually late, how many were caught. High recall means few missed late deliveries.
  • F1 — harmonic mean of precision and recall, useful because the two trade off.
  • ROC-AUC — probability that a randomly chosen late order is ranked above a randomly chosen on-time order. It measures ranking quality independently of the 0.5 threshold, which matters because the operating threshold is a business decision.
  • Confusion matrix — the actual counts behind the four metrics above.

7. Results and Discussion

7.1 Best and worst models

Gradient boosting is best on every metric (CV ROC-AUC 0.896, test 0.898, test F1 0.828). Logistic regression is worst (0.881 CV, 0.882 test).

The decision tree (0.889 CV / 0.891 test) and the random forest (0.890 CV / 0.889 test) sit between them and are effectively tied — the sign of their 0.001 difference flips between CV and test, which is what noise looks like at this sample size.

7.2 Why the models differ

  • The linear model is limited by its functional form. Logistic regression sees weather_severity and distance_km as separate additive terms, so it must average over their interaction — the weakness Section 3.4 identified. Its training and CV scores are identical (0.881/0.881), which is the signature of high bias, not overfitting: it cannot represent the pattern, so it cannot memorise it either.
  • The tree ensembles can represent the interaction without being told about it, because a split on distance can sit directly beneath a split on weather. That is worth 0.007–0.016 ROC-AUC over the linear model.
  • Boosting beats bagging here because the dataset is dominated by a few high-risk cells. Boosting re-weights errors sequentially, so later trees concentrate on the storm/long-haul cases that earlier trees under-predicted. Bagging averages independently fitted trees and has no mechanism to focus.
  • The single tree is competitive once regularised (max_depth=8, 20 orders per leaf). With 40,000 rows, a depth-8 tree already captures the structure; the forest’s variance reduction adds little because there is little variance left to remove.

7.3 Overfitting and underfitting

Model Train ROC-AUC CV ROC-AUC Test ROC-AUC Train − CV gap
Logistic regression 0.881 0.881 0.882 −0.000
Decision tree 0.901 0.889 0.891 0.012
Random forest 0.961 0.890 0.889 0.071
Gradient boosting 0.906 0.896 0.898 0.010
  • The random forest shows the largest training gap (0.961 vs 0.890). Its individual trees memorise their bootstrap samples, and the averaged training predictions stay optimistic. Its CV and test scores still agree (0.890 vs 0.889), so this is a training-score artefact rather than a generalisation failure.
  • The other three show small gaps (0.012, 0.010, −0.000), so neither the depth limits nor the boosting rounds let them run away.
  • No model underfits: every model beats the 0.563 majority-class baseline by a wide margin, and the fold-to-fold standard deviation is 0.003 for all four.
  • The test score is within 0.002 of the CV score for every model, which confirms the cross-validation estimate was honest.

7.4 Did tuning improve performance?

Barely, and that is the honest answer. The best configuration (learning rate 0.05, 15 leaves) raised CV ROC-AUC from 0.8964 to 0.8976 — +0.0012, less than half the fold-to-fold standard deviation. Test ROC-AUC was unchanged at 0.898 and test F1 slipped from 0.828 to 0.827.

Two conclusions follow. First, the model is limited by its features, not its settings: the remaining error comes from causes the dataset does not record (carrier execution, traffic, local disruptions). Second, the search cost 20 extra pipeline fits for no measurable gain, which is worth reporting alongside the result rather than hiding.

In operational terms: of 10,000 orders, the model catches 4,698 of the 5,632 late ones (83.4%) while raising 1,028 false alarms. Whether that balance is right depends on the relative cost of a missed late delivery and an unnecessary upgrade — a question the dataset cannot answer.

7.6 Limitations and possible improvements

  • One year of orders. Monthly late rates stay between 55.5% and 57.7%, so there is no seasonality to learn and no year-over-year drift to measure. The reported scores describe this 2026 sample; a later period could shift them.
  • The promise is partly circular. promised_delivery_days correlates 0.93 with actual_delivery_days, so the model partly learns “long promise → late”. That is operationally true but weaker as a causal story.
  • No cost matrix. Precision and recall are reported separately rather than combined into a business loss, so the 0.5 threshold is a default rather than an optimum.
  • A single split. One random 20% test sample gives one estimate; repeated nested CV would give tighter confidence intervals.
  • Four models, one tuned. The comparison is wide, not exhaustive. A neural network or a properly-tuned random forest could be added, and probability calibration would make the outputs directly usable as risk scores.
  • Threshold optimisation is the most promising operational improvement: lowering the 0.5 cut-off trades precision for recall monotonically and costs nothing to train.

8. Conclusion

The project set out to predict, at order time, whether an e-commerce order would miss its promised delivery date, using only pre-dispatch information. It achieved that:

  1. The data was cleaned and documented. 50,000 rows, zero duplicates, one structurally missing column, and eight post-outcome columns that had to be removed — delivery_delay_days alone reproduces the target perfectly (AUC 1.0000).
  2. The exploration found the real drivers. Weather (Snow 93.5% late vs Clear 35.0%), priority (Low 81.7% vs Urgent 25.7%) and distance (38.5% below 1,000 km vs 82.8% above 5,000 km), with weather and distance compounding — storm at 5,000+ km is 100% late.
  3. Four models were compared under identical, leak-free cross-validation. Gradient boosting was best (CV ROC-AUC 0.896), logistic regression worst (0.881); the tree and forest tied in between.
  4. Tuning gave a negligible gain (+0.0012 CV), showing the model is feature-limited rather than setting-limited.
  5. The final model achieved 0.898 ROC-AUC on untouched test data — 0.804 accuracy and 0.828 F1, against a 0.563 majority-class baseline — with preprocessing refitted inside every fold, so no test information influenced any decision.

Objectives achieved. The model ranks orders by lateness risk well above the baseline and is honest about its limits: it cannot explain delays caused by factors the dataset never records, and it should be calibrated and threshold-tuned before operational use.
